From Mining to AI Compute

A China infrastructure guide: what transfers from crypto mining, what doesn't, and what the numbers actually say.

The Opportunity

Crypto mining facilities in China have three assets that matter for AI compute:

  1. Power capacity with established utility contracts at competitive rates (e.g. 0.59 CNY/kWh in Hebei)
  2. Physical infrastructure: raised floors, cooling systems, backup generators, security
  3. Operator expertise: people who know how to keep a data center running 24/7

These three assets are worth more than the mining rigs themselves. The facility is the real value, not the chips inside it.

ASIC vs GPU: Why Miners Cannot Run AI

Critical fact: SHA-256 ASIC miners (e.g. Antminer S19) have no GPU, no VRAM, and no CUDA cores. They cannot run AI inference workloads. This is a hardware architecture limitation, not a software one.

The question is not "can the existing miners run AI?" The answer is no. The question is: "can we drop GPU servers into the existing facility and skip the buildout cost?"

What transfers: the facility, the power, the cooling, the operator's knowledge.

What does not transfer: the chips.

Existing equipment options

A

Keep mining (status quo)

Continue mining as long as it covers electricity. Risk: difficulty increases, equipment degrades, 1-2 years max before unprofitable regardless.

B

Sell miners, buy GPUs

Liquidate the ASIC fleet on secondary market (heavily discounted for 5+ year old hardware). Use proceeds as partial funding for GPU server purchases.

C

Gradual transition

Use mining cash flow to fund GPU purchases over time. Buy 5-10 GPU servers per quarter. Self-funding, no external capital, but slow ramp.

D

Hybrid floor

Keep miners in one section, install GPU servers in another. As miners become unprofitable, swap: retiring ~2 miners (~6kW) frees enough power for 1 GPU server (~5kW).

ROI Sensitivity: GPU Rental Payback

Payback period varies by three factors: utilization rate, hardware cost, and subsidy access. No marketplace achieves 100% utilization. Budget for 50-60%. VastAI verified hosts typically see 70-85%.

Token Selling Model

Instead of renting bare GPU hours, you can run inference servers and sell API access priced per token. This is what Together.ai, DeepInfra, and Fireworks do.

Think of it this way: selling GPU hours is renting out an empty room. Selling tokens is renting out a furnished apartment.

Token economics: worked examples

Small model (8B) on 1x H100, own facility (electricity only):

Tokens/month:     12,500 tok/s x 3,600 x 0.70 x 730 = ~23B tokens
Electricity/month: 3.5kW x 730hr x 0.59 CNY = 1,506 CNY ($209)
Revenue at $0.20/M output: 23,000M x $0.20 = $4,600/month
Gross margin: 95%+ (electricity is nearly free at Chinese rates)

Large model (70B) on 1x H100, own facility:

Tokens/month:     400 tok/s x 3,600 x 0.70 x 730 = ~736M tokens
Electricity/month: $209 (same)
Revenue at $0.30/M output: 736M x $0.30 = $221/month
Gross margin: still positive but tiny volume

Key insight: Token selling with your own hardware is profitable at any model size because marginal cost is just electricity (~$209/month). The question is volume and demand. Small models (8B) produce far more tokens per GPU-hour and generate higher total revenue.

Throughput estimates based on vLLM benchmarks and NVIDIA TensorRT-LLM.

Investment Options

Option 1: Facility-Only Play (Lowest Risk)

Lease the facility (power, cooling, space) to an AI compute company. Operator provides infrastructure; tenant provides GPU servers.

Investment: Near zero (facility already exists)

Revenue: Hosting fees, typically 0.1-0.3 CNY/kWh markup on power + space rental

Payback: Immediate (no hardware purchase)

Risk: Low. No GPU depreciation. Revenue is modest but predictable. This is a traditional colocation model.

Option 2: Small Test (10 Servers, Subsidy-Dependent)

Purchase 10 GPU servers. List on VastAI or similar marketplace. Test utilization rates for 3-6 months before scaling.

Investment: 2.1M-3.5M CNY before subsidy, 1.05M-1.75M CNY after 50% subsidy

Annual revenue (60% util): ~$273,000 (1.97M CNY) via GPU rental, or ~$552,000 (3.97M CNY) via token selling

Payback at 50% subsidy: ~1.8 years (GPU rental) or ~11 months (token selling)

Risk: Medium. GPU depreciation is real (3-5 year useful life). Utilization below 40% extends payback beyond GPU lifespan.

Key prerequisite: Verify subsidy eligibility before purchasing.

Option 3: Full Pivot (100 Servers, Marketplace Scale)

Convert the entire facility to GPU compute. Requires significant capital but achieves marketplace-relevant scale.

Investment: 21M-35M CNY before subsidy

Annual revenue (60% util): ~$2.73M (19.7M CNY) via GPU rental

Payback at 50% subsidy: ~1.8 years

Risk: High. Large capital outlay, GPU obsolescence risk, utilization depends on marketplace demand. At this scale, direct enterprise contracts (not marketplace) may offer better rates and utilization guarantees.

Option 4: Workflow Delivery (Highest Margin, Most Complex)

Instead of renting bare GPU hours, sell AI inference services (completed workloads, not raw compute). The margin on workflow delivery is 10-100x over commodity GPU rental.

Investment: Hardware + software platform development

Revenue per unit: Much higher (selling results, not hours)

Payback: Potentially under 1 year, but requires software capability

Risk: Highest complexity. Requires AI platform expertise to build and maintain the inference stack.

Option 5: Token Router (Asset-Light Middleman)

Instead of running AI yourself, operate as a compute intermediary. Buyers purchase tokens through your platform; you source compute from multiple GPU suppliers and take a margin.

Investment: Platform development costs only. No GPU hardware purchase required.

Revenue: 10-20% commission on each transaction

Payback: Depends on transaction volume

Risk: Low hardware risk (no GPU depreciation). Market risk depends on ability to aggregate demand. This is the model companies like inference.ai operate.

Subsidy Reality Check

The 50-70% hardware subsidy does not exist. What exists at those percentages are compute SERVICE rental vouchers (算力券), not hardware purchase subsidies. This is a critical distinction.

What actually exists: compute service vouchers (50-60%)

Hardware purchase subsidies: only ~10% where they exist

NVIDIA chips and Chinese subsidies are mutually exclusive. Most subsidy programs require or strongly favor domestic chips. Electricity subsidies in Gansu, Guizhou, and Inner Mongolia (up to 50% off power rates) explicitly exclude data centers using foreign chips. See China's approved domestic chip list.

The "50% subsidy" and "70% subsidy" columns in the payback tables above should be read as hypothetical best-case scenarios, not as currently available programs. The realistic hardware cost is the full China gray-market price (e.g. 700k CNY for B200) unless subsidy eligibility can be confirmed through a domestic chip pathway.

Locations

Tangshan (Hebei)

Example facility city. Advantages: existing mining infrastructure, 0.59 CNY/kWh industrial power, proximity to Beijing (90 min by rail). Limitations: not a designated East Data West Computing hub, no verified AI-specific subsidy program. Available incentives are general high-tech enterprise tax benefits (15% corporate rate vs 25%) and potential for ad-hoc negotiations with local government for significant investments. See Tangshan Big Data Plan.

East Data West Computing hubs (东数西算)

China's national framework routes compute demand from eastern population centers to western regions with cheaper power. The designated hub regions and their advantages:

  • Guizhou: Hydropower, cool climate, Apple/Huawei data centers already present. 10% hardware subsidy available (domestic chips). Power rates as low as 0.30-0.35 CNY/kWh. Guizhou compute incentive
  • Inner Mongolia: Wind + solar, extremely cheap land. Well-connected to Beijing via fiber. Power rates 0.26-0.35 CNY/kWh for qualifying facilities.
  • Gansu: Abundant renewable energy, government actively recruiting. Electricity subsidies up to 50%, but foreign chip exclusion applies.
  • Ningxia: Similar renewable profile to Gansu, designated national hub. Zhongwei is a major data center cluster.

Taizhou (Zhejiang)

Coastal alternative with different advantages: proximity to Shanghai's AI ecosystem, Zhejiang's strong tech policy environment, established manufacturing base. Power costs are higher (~0.70+ CNY/kWh) but latency to major markets is lower.

Location choice depends on the business model. For marketplace GPU rental (latency-tolerant workloads like training and batch inference), western hubs with cheap power win. For real-time inference serving east-coast customers, proximity matters more than power cost.

Cross-Border Considerations

Can China-based GPU farms sell compute internationally? This is the highest-value scenario, since international rates ($2-8/hr per GPU) far exceed domestic Chinese rates. But several factors limit it.

Technical constraints

  • Latency: China-to-US round-trip latency is typically 150-250ms. This is acceptable for training and batch inference but problematic for real-time applications. China-to-Southeast Asia is 30-80ms, a more viable market for real-time serving.
  • Great Firewall: International bandwidth is throttled and subject to deep packet inspection. VPN tunnels add overhead and introduce compliance risk. Dedicated cross-border lines (CN2, IPLC) are expensive and require telecom carrier agreements.
  • Bandwidth cost: International bandwidth from China is 5-10x the cost of domestic bandwidth. High-throughput workloads (large model weights, training data transfers) become expensive.

Legal and regulatory

  • Hardware provenance: H100/B200 GPUs are US export-controlled to China. Gray-market acquisition is how they arrive. Advertising these GPUs on international marketplaces creates visibility into the supply chain that operators may want to avoid. Recent policy: BIS H200 export policy shift, H200 shipments to China
  • Data sovereignty: China's data localization laws (PIPL, Data Security Law) restrict certain categories of data from leaving the country. International customers sending data to China-based GPUs may face compliance issues in their own jurisdictions.
  • Platform policies: VastAI and RunPod do accept hosts globally, but verification and trust scores are harder to achieve from China-based facilities due to network reliability concerns.

Realistic assessment: Cross-border GPU rental from China is technically possible but operationally complex. The strongest use cases are serving the domestic Chinese market, Southeast Asian customers with low-latency requirements, or operating as a Chinese-market-focused node in a global compute network.

What Not to Do

  1. Do not assume 100% utilization. No marketplace achieves this. Budget for 50-60%.
  2. Do not buy hardware before confirming subsidy eligibility. The subsidy is the difference between a 1.8-year and 3.6-year payback.
  3. Do not assume ASIC miners can run AI. They cannot. The facility is the asset, not the chips.
  4. Do not ignore GPU depreciation. A 3-year payback on hardware that becomes obsolete in 4 years means only 1 year of profit before the cycle repeats.
  5. Do not use a single number for ROI. Payback depends on utilization, subsidy, and purchase price. Use the sensitivity tables, not a single estimate.

Recommended Next Steps

  1. Verify current mining profitability. Is the facility cash-flow positive after electricity? If not, the pivot decision is already made.
  2. Investigate provincial AI compute subsidy policies. Application process, eligibility criteria, domestic chip requirements.
  3. Register on VastAI to understand verification requirements and current utilization data for the target GPU tier.
  4. Get a real quote on B200 or equivalent GPU servers through Chinese channels, including subsidy-eligible pricing for domestic alternatives (Huawei Ascend).
  5. Start with Option 1 or a minimal Option 2 (2-3 servers) to validate utilization rates before committing significant capital.
  6. Reassess in 3 months based on test utilization data.
