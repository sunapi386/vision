# The Distributed Architecture

In January 2025, OpenAI and its partners announced Stargate, with a [plan to invest $500 billion over four years](https://openai.com/index/announcing-the-stargate-project/) in US AI infrastructure. That is an announced plan, not money already spent. Either way, it makes one question hard to ignore: who will own the machines the agents run on?

I am betting on distributed ownership with interoperable protocols. Hospitals, firms, schools and individuals should be able to own useful AI capability and choose when to buy more. Centralized services will have a place. The future I want gives their customers the power to refuse terms and take their essential work elsewhere.

That bet puts a demanding obligation on the architecture: make ownership usable, make dependence visible and make exit work. A server under the desk is only the beginning.

## The Hardware Trajectory

The office manager's $5,000 appliance from the first chapter works because her task is bounded. Read an invoice, check it against the vendor list, flag the odd ones. A small specialized model can do that well. Hand the same box an unfamiliar reasoning problem and it may not cope.

That is the honest shape of local inference. It makes some forms of ownership practical today. Model quality, memory, throughput, energy and operating effort decide which workloads fit. Compare options on the quality and total operating cost the actual workload needs.

Training and inference use resources differently, and neither has a single permanent architecture. Models get updated and retrained. Inference ranges from a small local operation to a demanding distributed service. A forecast about cheaper chips does not, by itself, show that every enterprise workload will fit on an appliance by a particular year.

So the useful direction is to make owning and operating the right capability less work. A local appliance can manage model loading, routing, evaluation and maintenance for well-defined tasks. The organization still needs plans for updates, hardware failure, security and the cases the local system cannot handle.

Distillation and compact adapters are candidate ways to make specialized workloads cheaper. Test them on the intended task under real deployment conditions. Encoding a policy in model weights changes behavior, but it does not turn a learned judgment into an unbreakable rule. Separate permissions and approval mechanisms are still necessary.

A hybrid architecture lets an organization compare these options without rebuilding its workflows around each provider. Either way, the accountability record should say where each operation ran and which permissions allowed it, whether the compute was owned, rented or borrowed.

## The Mesh Network

Here is the catch with the box under the desk. If it dies, so does the AI capability. Cloud providers offer scale, redundancy and managed services. Run your own hardware and you manage your own hardware. The solo practitioner with a server under the desk is sovereign, and also fragile.

Sovereign doesn't have to mean isolated. A mesh connects independently operated nodes under explicit rules. Picture a law firm that processes contracts during business hours and offers its idle capacity overnight to a hospital running diagnostic workloads, under defined terms, with defined governance and full accountability tracking. The mesh handles the routing, the billing and the data isolation. The two organizations interact through protocol, not through trust.

Whether that works comes down to the details.

A mesh is only as resilient as its control plane, identity services, routing and recovery. Counting machines tells you little. A central dependency can survive even when the hardware is spread across a country. Distributed networks show that work can continue through some failures when routes, capacity and authority exist elsewhere. That has to be tested. A network partition, a shared software defect or a compromised update can hit many otherwise independent nodes at once.

Each participating organization should control its permitted workloads, data access and external connections. It also needs authority over updates and a way to keep operating when a provider is unavailable. Owning the hardware does not provide those controls by itself, and it does not remove the organization's legal and institutional obligations.

Recovery must respect the original permissions. Spare capacity in another region is not permission to move a workload there. If no authorized destination can run it, the system should pause or ask for a new grant. Reliability means keeping the constraints during a failure, not keeping the work running at any cost.

Location-aware scheduling can enforce destination restrictions when node identity and location are verified and every relevant path is controlled. That analysis has to include outputs derived from the data, logs, backups and support access. Residency is one part of governance. It does not replace the rest.

## The Marketplace as Ecosystem

The mesh creates the hardware substrate. The marketplace creates the economy that runs on it.

Consider what the App Store did for mobile computing. Before the App Store, building software for phones required a deal with the carrier, approval from the handset manufacturer, and distribution through a walled garden that extracted most of the value. The App Store didn't invent mobile software. It made distribution universal and let millions of creators participate. A teenager in Helsinki could build an app and distribute it globally, overnight, to every iPhone on the planet.

The workflow marketplace does the same thing for professional expertise.

An immigration lawyer who has processed five thousand TN visa applications knows things that no language model knows: which supporting documents the adjudicator at a specific port of entry looks for, which phrasing in the support letter triggers additional scrutiny, which qualifications map cleanly to the USMCA (formerly NAFTA) profession codes and which ones require creative interpretation. This knowledge is hard-won. It took years to accumulate. It lives in the lawyer's head, in a filing cabinet, in a set of templates that the lawyer has refined over a decade.

The workflow marketplace lets this lawyer encode that knowledge into an executable pipeline. Document intake. Eligibility screening against profession codes. Supporting letter analysis. Application assembly. Form generation. Human review at three checkpoints. Filing package production. The pipeline doesn't replace the lawyer's expertise; it is the lawyer's expertise, captured in executable form.

The lawyer publishes the workflow. An immigration firm in Vancouver discovers it, deploys it on their own local hardware, and processes their TN applications through it. The workflow handles the pattern-matching work. The Vancouver firm's lawyers handle the judgment calls. The original lawyer earns a fee for each execution. Passive income from expertise that would otherwise be limited to the clients she could personally serve.

Scale this across every profession that involves repetitive, judgment-intensive work. The accountant's SR&ED filing workflow. The hospital's triage protocol. The procurement officer's vendor evaluation pipeline. The HR department's hiring workflow. The compliance officer's regulatory screening process. Each one captures domain expertise that took years to develop. Each one runs on sovereign infrastructure under the deployer's control. Each one earns its creator income from the marketplace.

This is the App Store for work. Except the apps don't play games or edit photos; they encode human expertise into executable processes. And the "phones" aren't consumer devices but sovereign compute nodes running in offices, hospitals, government buildings, and server closets. The marketplace is how expertise scales beyond the individual practitioner, beyond the geographic market, beyond the number of hours in a day.

The economic structure reinforces the distribution model. Every workflow execution happens on local hardware. The marketplace handles discovery, distribution, and payment. The workflow creator earns 70-80% of the execution fee. The infrastructure layer takes 20-30%. No central server processes the data. No cloud provider sees the documents. The economics flow through the protocol, not through a data center.

## The Agent Economy at Scale

Project forward ten years. Inference hardware is ubiquitous. Mesh networks connect millions of sovereign nodes. Marketplaces distribute hundreds of thousands of specialized workflows. What does the economy look like?

When millions of agents transact across thousands of organizations, the accountability infrastructure becomes the fabric of the economy itself: the substrate on which agent commerce operates, not a product feature or a compliance add-on.

The analogy is financial infrastructure. SWIFT doesn't process payments; it provides the messaging protocol that banks use to communicate about payments. SWIFT doesn't hold money. It doesn't decide who can transact. It provides the standard that makes transactions between 11,000 financial institutions in 200 countries possible. Without SWIFT, international banking would require bilateral agreements between every pair of banks. With SWIFT, any bank can transact with any other bank by following the protocol.

The accountability protocol serves the same function for agent transactions. When a legal workflow in Toronto calls a translation agent in Montreal that calls a document analysis agent in Ottawa, the accountability envelope travels with the execution. Each agent records its costs, its provenance, its data governance compliance. The envelope aggregates across the chain. At the end, the client who initiated the workflow has a complete record: what was done, by which agents, at what cost, with what data, under what governance rules.

The analogy extends to DNS, the Domain Name System that translates human-readable web addresses into machine-readable IP addresses. DNS is invisible infrastructure. Nobody thinks about DNS when they type a URL. But without DNS, the internet doesn't function. It's the thin layer that makes everything else possible.

Accountability infrastructure is the DNS of the agent economy. When it works, nobody notices it. When it doesn't exist, nothing works, or rather nothing works at the level of reliability and trustworthiness that serious applications require. Agents can transact without accountability. They just can't transact in ways that satisfy regulators, insurers, auditors, and clients. Which means they can't transact in the industries where the most value lives: healthcare, finance, legal, government, defense, education.

The agent economy at scale also creates emergent behavior that no single node controls. Reputation scores accumulate from millions of transactions. A workflow with a 98% auto-approval rate and a 0.5% error rate across 50,000 executions has a track record that no marketing claim can match. The trust layer becomes a credit bureau for agents: not a rating someone assigns, but a statistical reality derived from observed performance. Buyers can evaluate workflows based on actuarial data, not promotional materials.

Pricing equilibrium emerges. If an SR&ED filing workflow costs $300 and a competitor publishes one at $200 with comparable quality metrics, the market adjusts. If the cheaper workflow has a higher error rate, the quality metrics reveal it. The marketplace creates price discovery for professional services that have never had transparent pricing, because the work was always bespoke, always opaque, always priced based on the provider's reputation rather than the outcome's measurable quality.

## The Sovereignty Question

Remember Alberta's Pronghorn from Part V: a government platform for sovereign AI, deployed on Render, which runs on Google Cloud. The government had the ambition and the design talent. The control sat with someone else.

Sovereignty means being able to exercise control. Having a server nearby is not enough. Ask four questions. Who can change the policy? Who can revoke a credential? Can the organization keep its work and evidence when it changes providers? Can it keep operating if the vendor disappears?

The same questions apply to marketplaces and reputation services. Compute can be distributed while discovery, pricing and identity stay concentrated. Open specifications need independent implementations, usable exports, transparent decisions and a route to challenge exclusion. Otherwise the dependency has moved. It has not shrunk.

Local processing can reduce exposure to outside providers. It does not make privacy or security automatic. Administrators, software updates, permitted outputs and physical access are still part of the system. Every guarantee should state its assumptions and be tested against the full path the data can take.

The business should win when organizations choose its service because it works well. Their accumulated labels, policy history and permitted calibration evidence should stay accessible and portable. The right to leave is the practical test of the sovereignty argument.

## The Question of Whether

None of this waits on a date for general intelligence. The capabilities that exist today already give organizations choices about how work gets done. What remains uncertain is how fast, how far and who benefits.

A plausible future mixes local capability, shared infrastructure and specialized outside services. Whether it is a good one depends on whether people can understand and influence those arrangements. Ownership widens their options. Interoperability and accountable institutions keep those options open.

The design task is to make delegation useful without making dependence irreversible. That means a workable path through ordinary operations, disagreement, failure, recovery and exit. Judge the protocol by how well it handles those paths.
