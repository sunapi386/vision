# The Future We Choose

In September 2026, I went for a walk with my son, who would turn eight later that month. We recorded the conversation. We talked about AI agents, the tools they can use, and who is responsible when one agent asks another to act.

He asked whether a parent agent could create a child agent with access to more powerful tools.

That is the delegated-authority problem from Part IV, in a seven-year-old's words. An agent should only pass along authority it actually has. Anything more needs a separate authorization. Creating another agent must never become a way around a restriction.

Later, when we got to approval and verification, he asked: "But what if the verification is wrong?"

That question belongs beside every safety diagram in this book. Recording an approval does not make it a good decision. Adding a reviewer gives us one more chance to catch an error, and one more decision whose quality needs examining. The chain has to end at people and institutions able to answer for what happened and do something about it.

We also reached ideas he did not yet understand. He tried analogies, made guesses, and compared himself to a baby trying to put a square into a circular hole. Those attempts matter to me. They are how someone learns to reason, including the confidence to offer an unfinished thought and revise it.

I felt proud of the time we spent thinking together. The walk also made the purpose of this work concrete. I want him to grow up able to understand powerful systems, question their rules and help change them. I want there to be room to learn, play, make things and spend time together without every activity needing an efficiency argument.

*This reflection summarizes the September 9, 2026 walk. The quoted question is transcribed from the recording; the surrounding discussion is condensed.*

## Good Records Are Not Enough

An organization can keep excellent records while pursuing a harmful goal. A community can own its computers and still have little say over the decisions made with them. An employee can get faster at reviewing AI output while losing discretion over their own work.

The architecture in this book is a proposal for making delegated work accountable. Its larger purpose is to give people more ability to shape their lives. The second does not follow automatically from the first. So the next design question is who gains the power to decide, and who can challenge that decision.

## Pick a Future

The earlier parts of this book point to five possible futures for the agent economy. I call them the Landlord, Monday Morning Archaeology, the Panopticon, the Patchwork and the Open Ledger. They differ less in technology than in two questions: who holds the records, and who can challenge a decision.

| Future | Who holds the records | Who can challenge a decision | What it gets right | What it costs |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------------|--------------------|---------------|
| **The Landlord.** A few providers rent intelligence by the token. | The provider, in its format, under its retention policy. | The customer files a support ticket and sees whatever the provider's interfaces show. | Access to frontier models, with no infrastructure to run. | Prices only move one way, switching costs compound, and the platform learns from your expertise. |
| **Monday Morning Archaeology.** Everyone deploys and hopes. | Nobody, as a whole. Fragments sit in scattered logs and billing dashboards. | Nobody can. The chain cannot be traced, and in court the missing record becomes evidence. | Speed. The productivity gains are real and immediate. | Runaway bills found on Monday, untraceable errors, and compliance that fails the first time a regulator asks. |
| **The Panopticon.** Receipts everywhere, with no limits on them. | Whoever runs the platform keeps everything, indefinitely. | The record is used to watch people. There is no route to appeal. | Every action is attributable. | The audit trail becomes a permanent archive of every personal interaction. |
| **The Patchwork.** Each vendor bolts on its own compliance, usually after a crisis. | Each vendor, in incompatible formats. | Inside one vendor's walls. The chain breaks at every organizational boundary. | Each platform can show a dashboard. | The compliance equivalent of the browser wars, built in a hurry and at far greater cost. |
| **The Open Ledger.** Owned and rented compute joined by an open accountability protocol. | Each party holds its portion, carried with the work and exportable when it leaves. | Affected people get reasons, a way to correct the record and an independent reviewer with a deadline. | Organizations can say no and keep working, and decisions can be contested. | The hardest to build. It needs independent implementations, tested exit, operating skill and institutions willing to maintain the standard. |

Which row would you pick?

You know mine. This whole book argues for the Open Ledger. The other four are not straw men, though. Each is where we end up if a particular choice goes unmade. Keep renting and you get the Landlord. Skip the records and you get Monday morning. Keep the records but skip the limits and the appeals, and you get the Panopticon. Wait for the first big failure and you get the Patchwork.

The rest of this chapter is about what the Open Ledger asks of us beyond the protocol.

## Expect an Uneven Transition

My expectation is that capability will spread faster than institutions adapt. Some workflows will become dependable quickly. Others will stay hard because the information is incomplete, the consequences are hard to reverse, or people disagree about what success means. Different sectors and communities will go through different transitions.

The division of labor will change too. AI may help with evaluation, planning and policy analysis as well as execution. Human authority should rest on responsibility and legitimate participation, not on a prediction that machines will always be worse at judgment. A person affected by a decision deserves a way to challenge it even if the system beats people on a benchmark.

Employment outcomes will depend on adoption, demand, bargaining power and policy as well as technical capability. The ILO cautions that exposure to AI is not itself a prediction of job losses. So treat workforce scenarios as possibilities to prepare for and outcomes to measure, not a promise that everyone moves into a better role. See the [ILO's explanation of exposure indicators](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/new-ilo-brief-explains-what-ai-exposure-indicators-reveal-about-jobs).

## Build the Right to Challenge

Imagine you are denied a service, and an automated system contributed to the decision. What should you be able to do?

You should be able to learn that a system was involved, see the relevant reasons, correct mistaken information, and reach a reviewer with authority to change the outcome. An appeal that goes back to the same model under the same instructions is not a real safeguard. Review needs an independent path, a response deadline and a named institution responsible for remedies.

This also changes how objectives get chosen. The organization paying for a workflow is not always the only party it affects. Workers, customers, patients and communities need ways to influence the rules for consequential uses. A policy change should have an identifiable author, an explanation and a record of objections and revisions. No universal score can settle a disagreement about whose interests count.

These requirements belong alongside the technical architecture. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework likewise treats feedback, appeal, override and engagement with affected communities as parts of risk management. See [AI RMF 1.0](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1).

## Preserve the Ability to Leave and Recover

Remember month twenty-four from Part I. The vendor raises prices 15 to 20%. Rebuilding elsewhere would cost $300,000 and three months of disruption, so the organization pays. Sovereignty is what lets you say no at that moment.

That means sovereignty has to survive a change of vendor. Organizations need usable exports of their workflows, permitted source records, policy history and calibration evidence. Another implementation should be able to verify those records and rebuild useful behavior, within privacy and licensing limits. Open formats need conformance tests and independent implementations before that promise is practical.

Reputation needs a route for correction too. A disputed score must carry the dispute with it. An old failure should not silently become a permanent verdict on every future task. Accountability requires proportionate retention and controlled access. It does not require collecting every private thought or keeping every underlying document forever.

Recovery has to work across the whole delegation chain. If an authorization is revoked, outstanding tasks need to stop or lose the affected capabilities. If a shared model or policy update causes a pattern of failures, operators need a way to pause dependent work, contain the damage and return to a known working configuration. Spreading out the hardware does not stop many nodes from making the same mistake.

Before a consequential action, the system should weigh the workflow's cumulative exposure: commitments already made, remaining budget, time left to intervene and consequences that cannot be undone. Many steps that are each permitted can add up to an unacceptable outcome. Receipts should help operators see that pattern forming while they can still act.

## Protect the Conditions for Human Judgment

Education needs protected time to practice. Learners should sometimes attempt the work before seeing the automated answer, explain their reasoning and get feedback on the difference. Organizations need to fund that time even when automating would be faster. Otherwise they use up expertise while weakening the path the next generation takes to acquire it.

The gains also need to reach people. Measure success in better service, affordable access, less unwanted administrative work and more control over time, alongside revenue and throughput. Shorter working hours, stronger public services, worker participation and support during job transitions are choices institutions can make. A technical platform can support and measure those choices. It cannot make them.

A good handoff makes the situation easier to understand: the decision, the evidence, the remaining uncertainty and the next available action, with the detail still reachable behind the summary. Fluent compression can hide an unresolved disagreement as easily as it can clarify an argument. The goal is to lighten the reader's load without removing what they need to question the result.

The future I want this infrastructure to support has more room for people to act on their own purposes. Some will build businesses. Some will do research, care for others, or make things for the pleasure of it. Some will take a longer walk with their children. The architecture earns its place by making those lives more possible, while keeping its own authority open to question.

## Coda: 2046

*Back to Lantern from the Prelude. This is the end of that story, and it is still fiction.*

In the spring of 2046, Iris Kowal climbed the escarpment stairs with her daughter on her back for the last forty steps, because Wren was five and had opinions about stairs.

The stairs were steep and wet from the night's rain, and the trees on either side were just starting to go green. Wren counted the last forty steps out loud, got lost somewhere in the thirties, and started again from twenty for safety.

At the top they sat on the stone wall at the brow and looked out. The lake was flat and silver. The bay below was still lined with old mill buildings, but most of them hummed now. Cooling vents stood where the stacks used to be. Hamilton made compute the way it used to make steel, and on a still morning you could hear it, one low note under the traffic. A light-rail train slid along King Street toward the east end, nearly silent, and somewhere behind them a dog was losing an argument with a squirrel.

Iris was twenty-seven. She was an auditor, which was not the job she would have predicted at eight, and exactly the job her mother had predicted.

Her mother had brought her up these same stairs once, a few days after the two offers, when Iris was fourteen. Claire hadn't said much. She had looked at the mills for a long time and then said, "I'm going to give it away." Iris had thought she meant the company. It took her years to understand she meant the format.

Lantern still had its second floor in the old mill, a little east of where they sat. It had sixty employees, about what it had had for a decade. It was a good small company that did careful work for hospitals. You would not have heard of it.

You would have heard of the format.

The first open version was clumsy. The second broke in a way that embarrassed everyone involved, including Claire, who said so in public. Eleven people left Lantern in those two years. Owen stayed, and complained, and fixed most of what broke. The third version held.

After that it spread the way boring things spread. The 2034 rules required run records for high-risk workflows, and regulators could read the open format without asking anyone's permission. By 2036, insurers priced workflows instead of companies. A workflow with a clean, checkable record could cost half as much to insure as one without, and that did more for adoption than any keynote.

By the early 2040s, run records were as dull as receipts. Every agent chain carried one. Nobody talked about them, the same way nobody talks about the numbers at the bottom of a grocery slip. Iris's job existed because they existed. She spent her days replaying other people's runs, looking for the step where something went wrong.

Last month it had been a benefits workflow up north. For six weeks, it had been quietly denying a certain kind of disability claim. Iris replayed four hundred runs and found the step: a model update had shifted a threshold, and the vendor's own evaluation hadn't covered the case. The record named the update, the date and the person who approved it. The vendor reopened 312 claims within a week, and a named official signed the letter that went to each one.

No dashboard had caught it. A person had, because the record let her look.

Some of the format's habits had come from people, not engineers. Ruth had retired years ago, to a house on the bay with a garden she complained about. Her line was in the spec. Every review screen built on the protocol had to show the case before it showed the score. Most people who used those screens had never heard of her.

And it reached people who would never read a spec. Wren's daycare used agents to handle billing and subsidy paperwork. Every parent could ask for the run record behind any decision about their account, and a handful did, every month. Usually nothing was wrong. Sometimes something was, and then there was a name on the record and a person who had to fix it.

What still surprised Iris, when she thought about it, was who had won.

In the early 2030s, everyone assumed the most powerful systems would be the most secret ones. Secrecy was the moat. The Clouds spent fortunes keeping their agents opaque, and for a while it worked. Then the Cascade made opacity expensive. After that, the actor people trusted most was the one they could check. Two of the three Clouds adopted the open format within five years, and their systems got better for it. The third held out, and got smaller.

Nobody ended up owning trust. You earned it one checkable run at a time, and anyone could look.

Claire never got rich. The nine-digit number became a family joke. She called it "the lunch," after the investor in 2027, and Owen still brought it up every Christmas, mostly to make her laugh.

It was not finished, either.

There were still walls. Some companies ran agents on systems that didn't speak the format at all, in places where nobody required it. Iris had followed chains that went dark at a border. And there were still people who got a decision and had nobody to challenge it with. The forms were too hard, the deadline too short, and the voice on the phone was an agent with no authority to change anything. The record existed. Nobody with power was reading it.

A receipt can show you what happened. It can't make anyone care.

Claire had said something like that at her retirement dinner, to a room that wanted a victory speech. "We built a better filing cabinet," she said. "Somebody still has to open the drawer."

On the way up, they had passed the mill. On its brick wall, next to the door, there was a small bronze plaque with twenty-three names on it and a date in March 2033. Claire had paid for it. Most people walked past without reading it. Iris always read it.

Wren had never known a world where a computer made a decision about you and kept no record. To her it would sound like a shop that didn't give change. Iris hoped it stayed that way, and knew it wouldn't stay that way by itself.

Wren was not interested in any of this. She was interested in the wall, and then in a crow on the railing, and then in Iris's work badge, which had a small lantern printed on it.

"Is that your job?"

"That's where Grandma worked. I check things for them sometimes."

"What things?"

Iris thought about how to say it to a five-year-old. "When computers do work for people, they write down every step. I read what they wrote, to make sure they did it right. Then I write down what I checked."

Wren looked at her with an expression Iris recognized from her own old photos. Then she asked it. Somehow Iris had known she would.

"But what if the verification is wrong?"

Iris laughed, the way her mother had laughed at a kitchen table nineteen years before, because it was a good question.

She thought of the sticky note. She had found it in a box when Claire retired, the ink almost gone, and had kept it in her wallet ever since.

"Then we can find out," she said. "I wrote down what I checked, and how. So somebody else can check the checking. And if I got it wrong, somebody has to answer for it. Maybe me."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and went back to the crow.

Iris stayed on the wall a while longer, looking down at the mills along the bay. Somewhere in a building that used to make steel, thousands of agents were working for people who would never think about them, and leaving receipts most of those people would never read.

That was fine. You don't have to read them. You just have to be able to.

## The Bottom Line

- AI changes tasks first. In every example in this part, the human role moved from producing the work to governing it.
- Trust is earned one task at a time, from evidence. A change in the model, the policy or the data can invalidate it.
- A gate can be enforced. A judgment can only be measured. Know which one you are relying on.
- Sovereignty is the ability to say no and keep working, which means being able to leave with your records.
- The technology does not pick the future. Who holds the records, and who can challenge a decision, decide which row of the table we end up in.
