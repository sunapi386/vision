# The Future We Choose

The architecture described in this book is a proposal for making delegated work accountable. Its larger purpose is to give people more ability to shape their lives. These are related ambitions, but the second does not follow automatically from the first.

An organization can keep excellent records while pursuing a harmful objective. A community can own its computers while having little say over the decisions made with them. An employee can become faster at reviewing AI output while losing discretion over their own work. The next design question is who gains the power to decide, and who can challenge that decision.

## A Question on a Walk

In September 2026, I went for a walk with my son, who would turn eight later that month. We recorded the conversation. We talked about AI agents, the tools they can use, and the responsibility that follows when one agent asks another to act.

He asked whether a parent agent could create a child with access to more powerful tools. That is the problem of delegated authority in everyday language. An agent should only pass along authority it has, and an expansion should require a separate authorization. Creating another agent must not become a way around a restriction.

Later, when we discussed approval and verification, he asked: "But what if the verification is wrong?"

That question belongs beside every safety diagram in this book. Recording an approval does not establish that it was a good decision. Adding a reviewer gives us another opportunity to catch an error, and another decision whose quality needs to be examined. The chain has to reach people and institutions able to answer for what happened and do something about it.

We also reached ideas he did not yet understand. He tried analogies, made guesses, and compared himself to a baby trying to put a square into a circular hole. Those attempts matter to me. They are part of how someone learns to reason, including the confidence to offer an unfinished thought and revise it.

I felt proud of the time we spent thinking together. The conversation also made the purpose of this work more concrete: I want him to grow up able to understand powerful systems, question their rules, and help change them. There should be room to learn, play, make things, and spend time together without every activity needing an efficiency argument.

## Expect an Uneven Transition

My expectation is that capability will spread faster than institutions adapt. Some workflows will become dependable quickly. Others will remain difficult because the information is incomplete, the consequences are hard to reverse, or people disagree about what success means. Different sectors and communities will experience different transitions.

The division of labor will change too. AI may assist with evaluation, planning, and policy analysis as well as execution. Human authority should rest on responsibility and legitimate participation, rather than a prediction that machines will always be worse at judgment. A person affected by a decision deserves a way to challenge it even if the system outperforms people on a benchmark.

Employment outcomes will depend on adoption, demand, bargaining power, and policy as well as technical capability. The ILO cautions that exposure to AI is not itself a prediction of job losses. For this book, that means treating workforce scenarios as possibilities to prepare for, with outcomes to measure, rather than a promise that everyone will move into a better role. See the [ILO's explanation of exposure indicators](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/new-ilo-brief-explains-what-ai-exposure-indicators-reveal-about-jobs).

## Build the Right to Challenge

A person denied a service should be able to learn that an automated system contributed, see the relevant reasons, correct mistaken information, and reach a reviewer with authority to change the outcome. An appeal that returns only to the same model under the same instructions is an inadequate safeguard. Review needs an independent path, a response deadline, and a named institution responsible for remedies.

This also changes how objectives are chosen. The organization paying for a workflow is not always the only party affected by it. Workers, customers, patients, and communities need ways to influence the rules that govern consequential uses. A policy change should have an identifiable author, an explanation, and a record of objections and revisions. No universal score can settle a disagreement about whose interests count.

These are design requirements for the proposed system, not claims that the current product implements them all. They belong alongside the technical architecture. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework similarly treats feedback, appeal, override, and engagement with affected communities as parts of risk management. See [AI RMF 1.0](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1).

## Preserve the Ability to Leave and Recover

Sovereignty should survive a change of vendor. Organizations need usable exports of their workflows, permitted source records, policy history, and calibration evidence. Another implementation should be able to verify those records and rebuild useful behavior, subject to privacy and licensing limits. Open formats need conformance tests and independent implementations to make that promise practical.

Reputation needs a route for correction too. A disputed score must carry the dispute, and an old failure should not silently become a permanent judgment about every future task. Accountability requires proportionate retention and controlled access. It does not require collecting every private thought or keeping every underlying document forever.

Recovery must work across the delegation chain. If an authorization is revoked, outstanding tasks need to stop or lose the affected capabilities. If a shared model or policy update causes a pattern of failures, operators need a way to pause dependent work, contain the damage, and return to a known working configuration. Distributing hardware alone does not prevent many nodes from making the same mistake.

Before consequential actions, the system should consider the cumulative exposure of the workflow: commitments already made, remaining budget, time available for intervention, and consequences that cannot be undone. Many individually permitted steps can produce an unacceptable combined outcome. Receipts should help operators see that developing pattern while they can still act.

## Protect the Conditions for Human Judgment

Education needs protected opportunities to practice. Learners should sometimes attempt the work before seeing the automated answer, explain their reasoning, and receive feedback on the difference. Organizations need to fund this time even when immediate automation would be faster. Otherwise they consume expertise while weakening the path through which the next generation acquires it.

The gains also need to reach people. Success should be measured in better service, affordable access, less unwanted administrative work, and more control over time, alongside revenue and throughput. Shorter working hours, stronger public services, worker participation, and support during job transitions are choices institutions can make. A technical platform can support and measure those choices; it cannot substitute for making them.

A useful handoff should make the situation easier to understand: the decision, the evidence, the remaining uncertainty, and the next available action. It should preserve access to the detail behind the summary. Fluent compression can hide an unresolved disagreement just as easily as it can clarify an argument. The goal is to reduce the reader's burden without removing what they need to question the result.

The future I want this infrastructure to support has more room for people to act on their own purposes. Some will build businesses. Some will do research, care for others, or make things for the pleasure of it. Some will take a longer walk with their children. The architecture earns its place by making those lives more possible, while keeping its own authority open to question.
