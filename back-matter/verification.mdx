## Scope and limits

The main text states each important caveat once, in plain words. The fuller limits live here, grouped by topic, so you can check exactly what the book claims and what it doesn't.

**Dates.** Facts and figures are as of the time of writing (May 2026). Some of them will date.

**What exists and what is proposed.** Some capabilities described in this book have implementations; others are design requirements. The seven-part model is a way to organize the work. It is not the only possible architecture, and the complete system does not exist yet. The commitments in Part VI (appeal and remedy, revocation and recovery, portability, protected time to learn) are design requirements for the proposed system, not claims that the current product implements them all. No system can guarantee good judgment.

**Confidence and calibration.** A model's stated confidence is not automatically a measured probability of being correct. A well-calibrated score describes a distribution of successes and failures; it does not certify an individual action as safe. Agreement between models is evidence to evaluate, not proof of independent judgment or calibrated correctness, because models can share training data, assumptions and blind spots. Adding judges is not a substitute for measuring whether the combined system improves decisions. Calibration has to be measured on held-out examples relevant to the deployment, with ongoing checks for changing inputs, model updates and systematic errors. Human reviewers also disagree and make mistakes: record their reasoning and later corrections, preserve an appeal path, and sample high-confidence outputs as well as flagged ones. An illustrative threshold for a low-stakes task such as document tagging cannot be reused as general permission to make consequential decisions.

**Enforced versus judged.** Explicit permissions, budget reservations and required approval steps can be checked by mechanisms outside the acting agent's control, within the system's defined boundary. Semantic judgments, such as whether an output is misleading or a disclosure is harmful, remain fallible. An enforced approval gate does not establish that the approver understood the decision or that the outcome was fair. Encoding a policy in model weights changes behavior; it does not turn a learned judgment into an unbreakable rule.

**Provenance.** Provenance gives a reviewer evidence to inspect: which inputs were available, which operations ran, and which sources are cited for a claim. A citation does not prove that the source supports the conclusion, and an execution trace does not reveal every cause of a model's output. Source matching, factual verification and judgment remain separate tasks.

**Reputation.** A larger history can improve an estimate when the observations are relevant and the outcomes are independently checked. Volume alone does not confer authority. A reputation record should identify the task, model and policy versions, evaluation method, sample size, uncertainty and disputed outcomes. Changes in deployment context can make an old record less informative. Existing evaluations and vendor dashboards provide pieces of this; by themselves they do not establish a shared reputation system.

**Sovereignty, privacy and security.** Physical custody can reduce dependence on an external operator, but it still relies on secure software, key management, administrators and controlled data paths; each deployment should identify those dependencies and state which protections it can actually verify. Local processing does not make privacy or security automatic. Encryption and organization-controlled keys restrict a provider's access only as far as the complete system does: plaintext can be exposed through execution, support access, logs or software updates, so a claim that the provider cannot read content needs a threat model that covers those paths and the authority of the control plane. An air gap removes network paths; removable media, physical access, maintenance and supply-chain changes still need controls, and an air gap is not proof that no information can ever leave. A mesh's resilience depends on its control plane, identity services, routing and recovery; a network partition, shared software defect or compromised update can affect many nodes at once. Location-aware scheduling enforces destination restrictions only when node identity and location are verified and every relevant path, including derived outputs, logs, backups and support access, is controlled. Not every hospital, government agency or financial institution requires local or disconnected processing, and running it responsibly takes maintenance, incident response and trained staff.

**Law and regulation.** Legal requirements vary by jurisdiction, data, purpose and sector. The regulatory examples in this book explain why organizations ask about control and evidence. They are not a universal requirement to own hardware, and current obligations need to be established for each deployment. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary guidance, not legislation. A protocol does not by itself establish legal compliance.

**Open protocols.** An open protocol permits independent implementations and verification. It does not eliminate trust in operators, software or key management. Practical portability needs conformance tests, migration exercises, versioning, long-term verification and institutions willing to maintain the standard.

**Hardware forecasts.** A forecast about cheaper chips does not by itself establish that every enterprise workload will fit on an appliance by a particular year. Options should be compared on the quality and total operating cost the actual workload requires.

**Work and employment.** Jobs are bundles of tasks, and task-level capability alone cannot tell us whether a role will grow, shrink or disappear. Exposure to AI is not itself a prediction of job losses (see the ILO explanation cited in Part VI). The shift from executing work to governing it is one important transition, not a universal one, and it is not guaranteed to preserve every job. The workforce examples are possible workflow designs, not claims that every organization has adopted them or that professional judgment is beyond automation. The workshops described in Part V show the needs I encountered; they do not establish that every program neglects these skills.

**Scenarios and convergence.** The Prelude and its closing bookend in Part VI are fiction: one scenario, not a prediction. The worked examples in Part VI (the law firm, police service, procurement office, small manufacturer and university) are scenarios with stated assumptions. The forces described in Part II create a strong reason to build compatible accountability infrastructure. They do not guarantee convergence on one stack, or on this one.
